# Presenting your Capstone 1 project

Everyone will have built an accounting ledger. Your presentation is your chance to show us what you made, how you got there, and what you learned.

## Timing

- Your presentation should last **7–10 minutes**.
- There is a **hard stop at 10 minutes**.
- After your presentation, there will be **up to five minutes for questions and answers**. Q&A does not count toward your seven-minute minimum.

Practice with a timer. Aiming for eight or nine minutes gives you some breathing room.

## Think about your audience

By the time you present, we may already have heard several versions of “This is my accounting ledger app. It reads a CSV file that looks like this.” Give us enough context to follow along, but keep the familiar introduction brief.

What will make your presentation interesting is your experience: the choices you made, the problems you worked through, and the things you learned. Think about what your classmates would find useful or interesting to hear.

We're humans. We like human stories. Your project didn't appear fully formed, so tell us a little about how you got from the assignment to the application you're showing us.

For example, your story might sound something like this:

> “For the first day and a half, I was confused and didn't make much progress. Then I talked it through with a classmate, and Dave helped me figure out where to start. Once I got the file loading, the rest started to make more sense.”

That's an example, not a story you need to copy. Tell your own story honestly. Asking for help, changing your approach, and getting past a confusing part are all worth talking about.

## Choose your visual aids

**Slides are optional.** You can use your README as a visual aid to give the presentation some structure and guide us through your project.

You can also use IntelliJ to run the application, walk us through interesting code, and answer questions. Choose a combination you're comfortable using, and practice switching between the things you plan to show.

## Run a demo you've practiced

You are expected to **run your application** so we can see it working.

Plan a short demo script: the sequence of screens, menu choices, and inputs you will use. Here, “script” means a plan you can follow; it doesn't have to be an automated script or a speech you memorize.

- Decide which features you want to demonstrate.
- Prepare the data and inputs you will use.
- Practice the whole sequence, including any setup or reset it needs.
- During the presentation, follow the sequence you practiced.

Don't make the presentation the first time you try a new input or explore a different path through the application. Murphy's Law loves a live demo.

## Show some code you're proud of

Your README should include some code you're particularly proud of. Make that part of your presentation, too. You can show the example in your README or open it in IntelliJ to walk us through it.

Help us understand:

- What does this code do?
- Why did you choose to show it?
- What was interesting or challenging about writing it?

It doesn't need to be the most complicated code in the project. A small piece that you understand well and can explain clearly can make a good example.

## Reflect on what you learned

Leave some time to talk about what you learned and what you would do differently next time. Those are useful parts of the presentation itself.

The questions afterward will generally be supportive. For example:

- What would you do differently if you started again?
- What was the most useful lesson you learned?
- How did you get past a problem you ran into?

You can return to your README, your running application, or your code in the IDE to help answer a question.

## One possible presentation plan

This is a suggested nine-minute outline. Adjust it to fit your project and your story.

| Time | What to cover |
| --- | --- |
| 0:00–1:00 | Briefly introduce your project and what you will show. |
| 1:00–3:00 | Tell your project story: how you started, what challenged you, and how you made progress. |
| 3:00–6:00 | Run your practiced demo. |
| 6:00–8:00 | Walk us through code you're proud of and explain your choices. |
| 8:00–9:00 | Share your lessons learned and what you would do differently next time. |

Rehearse the whole presentation, including the demo and the switches between your README, slides, and IDE. Check that you reach at least seven minutes and can finish comfortably before the ten-minute stop.
